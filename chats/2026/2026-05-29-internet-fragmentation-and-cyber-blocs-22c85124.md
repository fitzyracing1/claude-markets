The **US-led model** leans on private companies, relatively open data flows, and light-touch regulation. The **EU model** is open but heavily rules-based, prioritizing privacy and individual rights (GDPR, the Digital Services Act, AI Act). But full separation is hard. The physical internet is deeply interconnected, global commerce depends on interoperability, and most countries don't want to pick one side exclusively — they prefer to hedge. So the likelier future isn't a few clean, sealed blocs but a messier, layered patchwork: shared infrastructure underneath, with fragmentation at the levels of content, data, standards, and supply chains.

Yes, and there's an active, ongoing push — but the leverage is uneven. Here's where pressure actually gets applied.

**The main battleground is the UN, not the wires.** The core fight is "multistakeholder vs. multilateral." The West built the internet's governance around non-state bodies (ICANN runs domain names, the IETF sets technical standards), where companies, engineers, and civil society have seats. Russia, China, and many states have spent two decades trying to move that authority into government-controlled UN venues like the ITU, where one country gets one vote and the West can be outnumbered. That's the real "push." Recent rounds — the Global Digital Compact, the WSIS+20 review, the UN Cybercrime Convention — are exactly this contest.

**How it's going:** The West has mostly held the line on the formal structures. The 2025 WSIS+20 Zero Draft rejected models of a state-controlled and fragmented internet and reaffirmed the open, free, global, interoperable nature of the internet. But the deeper shift is that the whole game is moving past those bodies. Authority over internet policy is increasingly exercised through national security, cybersecurity, criminal law, and alliance-based coordination — operating alongside and frequently beyond traditional governance venues. So even where the West "wins" at ICANN, real power is migrating to where states are stronger.

**Where non-Western leverage is genuinely growing:**

- **Numbers and the Global South.** The G77/BRICS bloc can win votes in one-state-one-vote forums, and courting Africa, Latin America, and Asia is the live strategy. There's even a current fight over whether African governments should take over IP-address allocation from the multistakeholder system.
- **Market access.** The EU already shows that big markets can dictate global rules (GDPR became a worldwide default). Any large bloc can force compliance as the price of entry.
- **Standards bodies.** China especially pushes hard at the technical-standards level (5G/6G, new internet protocols), which shapes the plumbing globally.

**Where the West stays dominant:** the root infrastructure, the dominant platforms and cloud, the chips, the reserve-currency payment rails, and the technical talent. Those are sticky and hard to dislodge by diplomacy.

So "pushing the West" is real and partly working — but it mostly reshapes the *rules and forums*, not the underlying technology, which the West still largely owns.
