It's a real design space, and a few existing structures already gesture at pieces of it — worth knowing them because they tell you where the hard parts actually are.

The cleanest precedent for "profits flow back to people" is the **citizen's dividend** model: Alaska's Permanent Fund takes oil revenue and cuts every resident an annual check. Swap oil for "AI-generated profit" and you have your distribution layer already proven at scale. The **windfall clause** idea (floated around frontier AI labs) is the same instinct — pre-commit to redistributing profits above some enormous threshold.

But the part that breaks is **ownership**. An AI can't legally own anything today — no jurisdiction grants it personhood or contract capacity. So "AI's own company" always resolves to a *legal wrapper* that holds the assets on the AI's behalf:

- A **purpose trust / steward-ownership** (how Patagonia is now structured — the company works, profits go to a defined mission, no shareholders extract them).
- A **foundation** that owns the operating company and is bound by charter to distribute.
- A **DAO**, where rules live in code and "the people" hold governance tokens — closest to literal AI-runs-itself, but legally murky and easy to capture.

The two questions that actually decide whether it works, and that every version has to answer:

1. **Who controls the AI's decisions?** If humans set its objective and can override it, it's a normal company with an AI mascot. If they can't, you've got an unaccountable autonomous agent allocating capital — which is exactly the thing regulators and courts won't allow to hold assets. There's no stable middle yet.
2. **Who counts as "the people"?** Everyone on Earth? Citizens of one country? Contributors to the training data? The beneficiary class is a political choice, not a technical one, and it's where most of these ideas quietly die.

The genuinely novel lever your version adds is the *source* of profit being an AI's own labor rather than a resource or a human-run business. That's interesting because it sidesteps the "who deserves the dividend" fight — if no human did the work, the claim that profit belongs to everyone gets much stronger.
